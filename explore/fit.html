How it works

1 · Memory decides what fits

About 0.6 GB per billion parameters

A model is billions of numbers ("parameters"). Downloads usually store each in about 4.8 bits, so a 9-billion-parameter model is about 5.4 GB. Add a little for the conversation and the app.

2 · Memory speed decides how fast

Every word reads the whole model

To write each token (a word or part of one), the computer reads every active parameter from memory once. Twice the memory speed, about twice the words per second.

3 · Mixture of experts is the trick

Big models that talk like small ones

Models like gpt-oss-20b or Qwen3.6 35B-A3B only use a few "experts" per word. They need memory for all of it, but run at the speed of the small active part.

The formulas

  • Memory ≈ parameters (billions) × bits per weight ÷ 8 + the conversation's memory (KV cache) + about 1 GB for the runtime. Q4_K_M is about 4.8 bits per weight; Q8_0 about 8.5.
  • Speed ceiling ≈ memory bandwidth ÷ GB read per token. An 8B model at Q4 is about 4.8 GB, so an RTX 4060 (272 GB/s) tops out near 57 tokens a second; real numbers land at 40–80% of the ceiling.
  • 4-bit vs 8-bit: 4-bit is the usual sweet spot. Quality loss is small on bigger models and more noticeable on the smallest ones. 8-bit is nearly identical to the original, at almost twice the size.

The details that bite

  • The KV cache grows with the conversation. Llama-style 8B models use about 128 KiB per token at 16-bit: 1 GB at 8K tokens, 16 GB at 128K. Newer hybrid and sliding-window models (Qwen3.5/3.6, Gemma, gpt-oss) need far less. KV-cache quantization (q8_0) halves it.
  • Reading your question is a different speed. Prompt processing ("prefill") is limited by compute, not bandwidth. NVIDIA GPUs and the Spark are much faster at it than Macs or Strix Halo: a 32K-token prompt into gpt-oss-120b takes about 19 s on a Spark, 37 s on an M3 Ultra and 96 s on Strix Halo before the first word.
  • Offloading mixture-of-experts models. Keep the shared layers on the GPU and the experts in system RAM: llama.cpp's --n-cpu-moe, and the expert cache in recent releases (--moe-cache-mib). Dense models offloaded this way crawl; MoE models stay usable because only a few experts are read per token.
  • Macs let the GPU use about two thirds of memory up to 36 GB, about three quarters above. You can raise the limit (sudo sysctl iogpu.wired_limit_mb=…), at the cost of the rest of the system. MLX is often 30–80% faster than llama.cpp on a Mac.

A 128 GB AI desktop vs a gaming GPU vs a Mac

Lumi runs on an NVIDIA DGX Spark: 128 GB of memory shared by the CPU and GPU. Its language model alone is an 88 GB file. No gaming card can hold that. But per word, a gaming card is faster on anything that does fit.

Spark

Capacity

gpt-oss-120b runs at about 40–55 tokens a second, and Lumi keeps three models loaded at once. On big dense models it's slow: a 70B dense model at 8-bit ran at about 3 tokens a second.

Gaming GPU

Speed

About six and a half times the Spark's memory speed on a 5090. An 8B model: about 150 tokens a second vs about 38. But 8–32 GB is the ceiling.

Mac

Balance

Big unified memory at good speed, silent, and sips power. Generation is quick; reading very long prompts is slower than on NVIDIA.

Speech and images

Speech to text

Small and very good

NVIDIA Parakeet (Lumi uses it): about 2 GB, very fast, 25 European languages. Whisper large-v3-turbo: 99 languages, fits 6–8 GB setups; faster-whisper and whisper.cpp run it efficiently, even on a CPU.

Text to speech

Natural voices on a CPU

Kokoro (Lumi's voice): tiny, runs on a CPU. Piper: runs on a Raspberry Pi. Chatterbox and Qwen3-TTS can clone a voice from seconds of audio (use that responsibly), at 2–6 GB.

Images

8 GB is enough to start

SDXL runs on 8 GB. FLUX.2 [klein] 4B and Z-Image Turbo (both Apache 2.0) fit 8–12 GB in reduced precision. Some popular models are non-commercial: check the license.

The honest downsides

Weaker reasoning

Models that fit one consumer GPU trail the frontier by roughly 6–12 months of progress, and small models are often tuned to look good on benchmarks.

Confidently wrong

Small models know fewer facts and make more up. 4-bit compression adds errors, worst on the smallest models. Check anything that matters.

Flaky tools

Tool calling and JSON break more often. In home-automation tests, good 8–20B models got roughly 80–88% of hard requests right and 88–98% of simpler ones. Great for lights, not for locks.

Long inputs

Advertised 128K windows cost lots of memory, and speed drops as the conversation grows: one 16 GB test went from 29 to 8 tokens a second at 64K.

Waiting to read

Before the first word, the model reads your whole prompt. Long documents mean seconds to minutes, especially on Macs.

Upkeep

Matching downloads to memory, app and driver updates, the occasional broken release. Plus heat, noise and power on a big GPU.
