Part 2

It worked on my desk.

Then she met a crowd, an air conditioner,
a stage, and a reboot.

01

The 21-second hello

Nobody waits 21 seconds at a kiosk.

What happened

The model re-read her whole cheat sheet, about 13,000 words, for every visitor.

The fix

One identical prompt, warmed at startup, cached in the right place: 21.6 s → 0.5 s.

The lesson

Speed comes from not doing the same work twice.

02

The room is part of the system

A crowd, then the air conditioning.

What happened

In a crowd she answered strangers. When the AC kicked on, the background noise doubled.

The fix

Thresholds that follow the room, tuned on 75 s of recorded crowd noise. A shotgun mic, back to the crowd.

The lesson

Measure the real place, not your desk.

03

Two AIs on one stage

She answered the other assistant.

What happened

Another AI spoke through the room speakers. Lumi heard it and answered.

The fix

A “stay quiet” rule, plus a loudness fingerprint: speakers are loud 30–57% of the time, a person ≤ 13%.

The lesson

Tell the model the rule. Then check in code for when it misses.

04

The computer that turned itself off

Twice. No logs. No warning.

What happened

Sustained AI load, likely a heat spike the operating system never saw. Hard power-off.

The fix

GPU clock capped at boot, better airflow, one test request at a time: ~10 °C cooler.

The lesson

Heat and power are part of your design.

05

Confidently wrong

A bug that passed eleven reviews. A setting the AI got wrong.

What happened

Lip sync broke on long replies through 11 reviews. AI advice on a mic switch made it 10× too loud.

The fix

A review of the whole branch, not just each piece. Measure before you believe.

The lesson

You are the judge. AI can be confidently wrong.

06

Every restart is a new day

Muted speaker. Missing device. Wrong display.

What happened

After reboots: speaker at 0%, a USB speaker that dropped (27 restarts), a screen on the wrong display.

The fix

Set everything at startup. Wait out a missing device. Find what's really there.

The lesson

Rehearse the reboot, not just the demo.

Local and frontier: both have their place.

Frontier · Claude, GPT, Gemini

When the task is hard

  • Hard reasoning and analysis
  • Coding agents that work for an hour
  • Reliable tool use over many steps
  • Long documents, current knowledge
Local · on your machine

When the data or setting matters

  • Private: nothing leaves the machine
  • Offline: plane, basement, kiosk
  • No per-use cost, no rate limits
  • Yours forever: the model never changes

The honest downsides.

Weaker

Reasoning

Models that fit one gaming GPU trail the frontier by roughly 6–12 months.

Confident

Made-up answers

Fewer facts, more guessing. 4-bit squeezing adds errors. Check what matters.

Flaky

Tool calling

~80–88% of hard home requests right: great for lights, not for locks.

Slow

Long inputs

It reads your whole prompt before the first word. Long documents mean waiting.

Memory

Short conversations

A 128K-token window costs gigabytes; speed drops as the chat grows.

Upkeep

It's yours to run

Updates, drivers, the odd broken release. Heat and noise on a big GPU.

Home Assistant

On a small always-on box: a Green, a Raspberry Pi 5 or a mini PC.

Your model

On the gaming PC or Mac you already have, via Ollama or llama.cpp.

Not for locks

Good 8–20B models: ~80–88% of hard requests, up to 98% of simple ones. Confirm risky actions.

A weekend project.

  1. Install Ollama on your PC or Mac: ollama run qwen3:8b
  2. Install Home Assistant on a small always-on box
  3. Add the Ollama integration, turn on “prefer handling commands locally”
  4. Expose 5–10 devices with clear names. No locks yet.
  5. Type, then talk: the phone app, or a small voice speaker
Three guardrails
  • Locks and garage doors: confirm first
  • Never put the model server on the internet
  • Calendar text and email can carry hidden instructions
